A recipe book app

How MyRecipeBook works

A recipe book that lives on your phone. It reads recipes from websites, PDFs, photos of handwritten cards and recorded phone calls, and keeps them in one place you can search, cook from and share in English or Vietnamese.

The pieces

Four places, one recipe book

Nothing runs on a server anyone has to keep switched on. The app is split across three online services that cost nothing at this size, plus the PC where it's built.

Vercel

Runs the app and sends pages to your phone over a secure connection. Also keeps the loading page, styles and icons on fast servers near you.

Neon

The database. Holds every recipe, dish photo, photo of an original card, recording, transcript, translation and the search data.

OpenAI

The language model. Reads recipes out of pages and photos, transcribes recordings, translates, and understands what a search means.

A PC at home

Where the code lives and changes are tested. Sending a change to GitHub puts it live about a minute later.

Behind the scenes

Measured, not guessed

Why the first open takes a few seconds

To stay free, the app and the database go to sleep when nobody is using them. After a quiet spell, the first open takes about 6 seconds while they wake up. A loading page with a spinning circle shows straight away so the screen is never blank. Once awake, a page loads in about a sixth of a second.

machine 2 s app's code 3 s db 1 s
  • ~2 s Vercel finds and starts a machine to run the app.
  • ~3 s The app loads its code from storage. It takes 0.4 s on a laptop; cold storage is slower.
  • ~1 s The Neon database wakes and answers its first question.

Looking after it

Kept safe, kept cheap

  • Backups. Regular backups, plus instant snapshots before risky changes.

  • Signed in, private. The book sits behind a password, and its address and photos aren't public. Passwords and keys live in Vercel's settings, never in the code.

  • Costs. Hosting and the database are on free plans. OpenAI charges a cent or two per recording and less for links and photos. Recipes, photos and recordings are stored small: the whole book uses about 1% of Neon's free space.

  • Changes go live by themselves. Every change is tested on the PC first; sending it to GitHub makes Vercel build and switch to the new version about a minute later. A broken version can be rolled back in one click.

How it came to be

From a terminal script to a phone app

  1. Feb 2026

    A proof of concept

    Give it a recipe link and a language model pulls out a clean recipe. It ran in a terminal window.

  2. Spring 2026

    A real recipe book

    Reliable importing from links and PDFs, a test set to compare AI models, a proper database, and search that understands meaning as well as words. Then a phone-friendly app with a cooking view: tick off steps and keep the screen on.

  3. Summer 2026

    Recipes from phone calls

    Record a phone call, and the app transcribes the Vietnamese, writes the recipe in English and keeps the cook's tips. Recipes can be sent back in Vietnamese through Messages. Photos of handwritten cards are kept beside the recipe.

  4. 3 Nov 2026

    Online, for free

    Until then it only worked on home Wi-Fi with the PC switched on. Vercel won over Render, which would have cost about $7 a month. Neon was picked because it speaks the same database language the app already used, and both sit in Oregon, next to each other.

  5. 4 Nov 2026

    Faster, and pixel art

    A loading page for the wake-up, taps that only wait for the page itself, floating Back and Top buttons, and a pixel-art picture for every recipe without a photo.
